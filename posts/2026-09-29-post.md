What if an AI could scan hundreds of thousands of biological sequences and surface a discovery that human scientists hadn't noticed yet?

That's exactly what happened. Claude analyzed over 200,000 reverse transcriptase proteins found in bacteriophages — and autonomously identified a previously uncharacterized enzyme system called array-associated reverse transcriptases (ART). The system features evenly spaced DNA repeat sequences resembling CRISPR patterns, a structural clue that could open new doors in biotechnology and gene-editing research.

The work was validated by Feng Zhang of MIT and the Broad Institute, who noted it demonstrates "how AI agents can contribute to biological discovery."

This isn't AI replacing scientists — the wet lab work was done entirely by human researchers. But it does illustrate a new model: AI handling the scale of analysis that's simply beyond human bandwidth, then handing off the most promising leads for human expertise to pursue.

We're still early, but the trajectory is clear.
